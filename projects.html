RISE · Feb–Apr 2026

Getting eight bankers to show up

Lead Outreach Coordinator

A student-run panel only works if the panelists are people students actually want to hear from. Nobody at Goldman or Evercore owes a sophomore a favor, so the whole thing came down to how much outreach I was willing to do and which names I spent it on.

I treated it as a pipeline rather than a guest list. I worked through roughly 1,300 people, weighted toward alumni and analysts two or three years out, on the theory that a reachable associate is worth more than an unreachable managing director. Eight said yes.

The part I underestimated was everything after the yes. Confirming logistics, keeping people warm for six weeks, and making the night worth their evening took more work than getting the first reply did. The satisfaction scores came out of that, not out of the booking.

IKONA Partners · Jan–May 2026

Learning to kill names quickly

Investment Banking Spring Analyst

The job was finding enterprise software companies worth acquiring. The hard part is not finding companies. It is throwing most of them out fast enough that you still have time to look properly at the few that survive.

I screened on what showed up in the numbers rather than on how a company presented itself: growth you could see, a P&L that made sense, a business driver I could state in one sentence. Anything that needed a story to look good got cut. Running that screen across a thousand names is mostly an exercise in not getting attached.

Sourcing turned out to be a discipline problem more than a research problem. It is easy to keep a name alive because it is interesting to read about. The pipeline only works if you are willing to say no to companies you like.

Eos AI (YC W26) · Sep–Dec 2025

The forecast a company raised on

Founding Strategy & Operations Lead

I was the first business hire at a healthcare imaging startup, which in practice meant I owned the model. The deck we took into seed conversations sat on an operating model and a burn forecast I put together.

The useful part was watching which assumptions got challenged. Investors did not argue about the revenue line. They argued about the inputs underneath it: how long a pilot takes to convert, what a radiology group will actually pay, how many we could reach in a quarter. A forecast is a stack of those, and every one is a claim you have to defend.

It is also where I learned how quickly a model drifts. The numbers we wrote in September described a company that no longer existed by December. That distance between the forecast and the business is the part I find genuinely interesting, and it is most of why I want to end up on the investing side.
